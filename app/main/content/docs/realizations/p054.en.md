# Transforming quality control of an industrial line through a multidisciplinary Task Force

## About the Project

On an industrial line for processing metal products, recurring appearance defects led to quality losses and disrupted production performance.

The difficulty did not reside solely in the technical understanding of the phenomenon.

**Each production team felt they generated relatively little non-conformity. Yet, the accumulation of these defects across teams represented a significant volume, whose magnitude only became apparent at the end of the campaign.**

This late visibility limited the ability to react quickly. Information from incidents did not circulate sufficiently between teams, which favored the repetition of situations already encountered.

The quality control system also presented fragilities: insufficient capitalization of incidents, sometimes inconsistent documentation, standards to be strengthened, and imperfect articulation between different functions.

To address the problem in depth, a **multidisciplinary Task Force** was constituted and mobilized for several weeks.

The objective was to sustainably transform the operation of the line on this particular quality issue, acting simultaneously on the process, work practices, and operational management.

## Objectives

The Task Force pursued several complementary objectives:

- Reduce appearance defects and associated quality losses.
- Make non-conformity visible throughout the campaign, not only at its conclusion.
- Improve information flow between production teams.
- Avoid repetition of already identified incidents.
- Strengthen monitoring standards and reactions to deviations.

## Approach

### 1. Mobilize a dedicated Task Force for line transformation

The project was based on a temporary team, mobilized for several weeks and bringing together different skills: production, process, quality, and maintenance.

This organization allowed addressing the problem beyond usual boundaries between services.

The challenge was to produce a concrete transformation of operational functioning, rather than a new analysis report.

### 2. Make cumulative non-conformity visible between teams

One of the most important findings concerned the perception of quality losses.

Individually, the quantities of downgraded products by each team might seem limited. But their accumulation over a campaign represented a much larger volume.

**This difference between local perception and global performance was a major obstacle to controlling the problem.**

The Task Force therefore integrated the need to improve defect tracking and information transmission between teams.

The objective was to enable operators and management to have a shared view of the situation, identify repetitions more quickly, and react before losses accumulated.

### 3. Analyze technical causes and operational practices

As a process expert, I participated in the diagnostic work on the process and the analysis of known causes of the defect.

The investigations focused particularly on:

- Technical mechanisms capable of causing defects.
- Process monitoring conditions.
- Operating practices.
- Reactions initiated upon the appearance of a deviation.
- Capitalization of previous incidents.
- Consistency between risk analysis tools and documents used on the shop floor.

This approach allowed bringing together the physical understanding of the phenomenon and how it was actually controlled by the teams.

### 4. Align the control system

I notably participated in a workshop mapping the control plan based on known causes of the defect.

This collective work highlighted gaps between several components of the system:

**FMEA → Control Plan → Operating Standards → Reaction Sheets**

A cause identified in the FMEA had to be reflected by appropriate monitoring, understandable instructions, and an operational reaction in case of drift.

When these elements were insufficiently linked, the system could allow situations already analyzed to persist.

The Task Force work therefore aimed to strengthen this continuity and simplify documents to make them more useful on the shop floor.

### 5. Strengthen information transmission and capitalization

The repetition of incidents highlighted a need for better knowledge transmission.

**The objective was that each new incident enriches collective knowledge and contributes to avoiding its repetition.**

### 6. Establish sustainable improvement

The Task Force was not meant to solve only a one-off situation. Actions were integrated into the normal operation of the line.

## My Role

I participated in the Task Force by bringing my **process expertise** and knowledge of industrial mechanisms underlying defects.

My contributions notably included:

- Analysis of potential causes of the defect.
- Participation in diagnosing line operation.
- Mapping of the control plan.
- Identification of inconsistencies between FMEA (AMDEC), monitoring, standards, and reaction sheets.
- Incident capitalization work.
- Analysis of operating practices.
- Collective construction of a more coherent control approach.

This intervention was part of a multidisciplinary team mobilized to transform line practices.

## Results

**A collectively measured improvement in quality performance.**

Reduction of **34% of the defect**.

The project also enabled structuring several improvement axes:

- Better visibility on defects and their accumulation.
- Improved incident capitalization.
- Enhanced standards and reaction sheets.

## Lessons Learned

**An industrial quality issue can be exacerbated by information fragmentation, even when each team believes it controls its own contribution.**

This experience highlighted four essential principles.

**Make global performance visible.** The sum of small local losses can represent a major industrial challenge. Teams must be able to perceive the cumulative consequences of their decisions.

**Link control tools to the shop floor.** FMEA (AMDEC), monitoring plans, standards, and reaction sheets must form a coherent and operational system.

**Transform practices, not just documents.** Sustainable improvement requires team ownership, appropriate training, and regular follow-up.

**This project illustrates my ability to bring process expertise within an industrial transformation Task Force, contribute to a cross-functional approach to quality issues, and link technical causes to the organizational and managerial dimensions of performance.**
