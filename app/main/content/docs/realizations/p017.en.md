# Commanding in Emergency Situations: 15 Years of On-Call Duty, POI Drills, and Industrial Interventions

## Context

For **15 years**, I held on-call responsibility for production and maintenance at an industrial site.

This role exposed me to numerous unforeseen situations, often at night or on weekends, requiring rapid assessment, team coordination, and operational decisions.

I also participated in many fire drills organized under the **Internal Operation Plan (POI)**, holding an active role at the Advanced Command Post as part of my production responsibilities.

These experiences taught me to exercise command in circumstances where information is incomplete, stakes are high, and reaction time is limited.

They profoundly shaped my conception of operational management.

## Objectives and Responsibilities

- Ensure continuity of industrial operations outside working hours.
- Rapidly assess abnormal situations and their consequences.
- Coordinate initial decisions with other on-call managers.
- Provide decision support to teams present in the field.
- Participate in the command chain during POI drills.
- Organize the reporting of events and remaining actions.
- Maintain a relationship of trust with operational teams.

## Approach and implementation

### 1. Managing Production and Maintenance On-Call Duties

For fifteen years, I held on-call responsibilities covering production and maintenance activities.

Calls could concern technical incidents, production difficulties, or situations requiring coordination between multiple functions.

My role consisted of quickly understanding the problem, assessing its consequences, and organizing the response with present teams and other relevant on-call managers.

These interventions frequently took place under particular conditions: reduced staffing, limited access to certain contacts, partial information, and the need to make decisions without waiting for the usual organization to return.

### 2. Systematically Going to the Field

I adopted a personal rule: **for every on-call call and every drill, I go to the field.**

This approach stems from a simple conviction: an industrial situation cannot always be correctly assessed remotely.

The phone transmits information, but it does not necessarily convey the magnitude of an event, its environment, its risks, or the state of the teams.

By going on-site, I can confront explanations with observable facts, dialogue directly with operations staff, and better understand the difficulties they encounter.

This presence also allows me to fully assume my responsibilities and not leave teams alone facing a delicate situation.

**My method: observe, listen, understand, then decide.**

### 3. Participate in command during POI exercises

I have participated in numerous fire drills organized under the site's Internal Operation Plan.

At the Advanced Command Post, I played an active role in the command chain in my capacity as production staff.

These exercises enabled me to work on coordinating responders, information flow, and decision-making in simulated emergency situations.

They also taught me the importance of clear responsibilities and the quality of exchanges between all mobilized actors.

### 4. Decide with incomplete information

An on-call situation rarely begins with a complete view of the problem.

At the time of the first call, information may be fragmented, some consequences still unknown, and initial hypotheses uncertain.

I learned not to wait until I know everything before acting.

My approach consists of identifying available facts, assessing risks, taking the necessary first decisions, and quickly re-evaluating the situation when new information emerges.

**Commanding in uncertainty does not mean claiming to have full control, but rather deciding based on facts, clarifying priorities, and knowing how to revise decisions.**

### 5. Build a relationship of trust with operations staff

The quality of an emergency response depends directly on the relationship between the on-call manager and field teams.

I have always considered it essential to listen to operations staff, respect their expertise, and understand their constraints before deciding.

This attitude promotes more direct and complete information flow upwards.

On-site presence also contributes to creating a climate in which difficulties can be expressed without being minimized.

Over time, this trust becomes a genuine operational lever: exchanges are more effective, decisions better understood, and coordination smoother.

### 6. Manage weekend-specific situations

Weekend on-call duties present an additional difficulty.

Teams returning to work on Monday morning may not necessarily be aware of incidents that occurred, decisions made, or ongoing difficulties.

The organization's usual functioning can then slow down the resumption, particularly when information has circulated only among those mobilized during the weekend.

I learned to anticipate this break in continuity.

It is essential to make explicit the events, decisions, residual risks, and remaining actions.

### 7. Never leave an intervention without a review

I gradually established a second fundamental rule: **never depart without conducting a precise review of the situation.**

This discipline prevents considering an intervention complete simply because the immediate situation has been stabilized.

It contributes to ensuring continuity between on-call duty, operations teams, and management resuming their functions.

## My Role

**For 15 years, I held on-call responsibilities in production and maintenance, coordinating with teams and other managers to resolve numerous industrial situations.**

I also actively participated in the Advanced Command Post during POI fire drills.

These responsibilities were exercised within a collective framework, aligned with site procedures and other mobilized functions.

## Methods and Skills

**Areas:** industrial production, maintenance, incident management, on-call duty, POI drills, operational coordination.

**Methods:** field observation, situation assessment, prioritization, interfunctional coordination, decision re-evaluation, intervention review, instruction transmission.

**Skills:** operational leadership, decision-making under pressure, listening, communication, uncertainty management, trust, responsibility, operations continuity.

## Results

- **15 years of on-call experience** in production and maintenance.

- **Dozens of nights and weekends** dedicated to managing industrial situations, **Hundreds of problems resolved collectively** with operations teams and other managers.

- Participation in numerous POI fire drills, with an active role at the Advanced Command Post.

- Development of a personal method based on field presence, trust, progressive decision-making, and rigorous instruction transmission.

These results reflect long-term operational experience, without attributing collectively achieved resolutions to a single individual.

## Lessons Learned

### 1. Nothing replaces being on the ground

A phone report never provides as complete a view as direct observation.

Moving to the site allows for better appreciation of facts, risks, and real difficulties.

**The field remains the starting point of my decisions.**

### 2. Trust is an operational lever

A team that feels heard and respected more easily shares important information, including bad news.

This trust is built over time, through the behaviors and decisions of the leader.

It is not decreed: it is built intervention after intervention.

### 3. Commanding consists in acting despite uncertainty

At the beginning of an event, we almost never have all the information.

One must know how to decide based on available elements, while remaining ready to quickly modify one's assessment.

The important thing is not to defend one's first decision, but to evolve the response according to the facts.

### 4. The quality of listening conditions the quality of decisions

Operational staff possess irreplaceable knowledge of the equipment and situations they encounter.

The leader must rely on this expertise to build an appropriate response.

### 5. An emergency does not end with the disappearance of the immediate problem

Stabilizing the situation is not enough.

The causes to investigate, the consequences to address, and the actions to continue must be identified and communicated.

**Never leaving without a precise report is an essential rule of operational continuity.**

### 6. Weekends reveal weaknesses in handover

When regular teams return after an incident, the lack of structured information can slow down production resumption.

Good on-call management must therefore prepare for resumption through normal organization.

### 7. Leadership is built in difficult situations

Nights, weekends, and emergency situations reveal the quality of working relationships.

Presence, availability, respect for teams, and the ability to take decisions gradually build a leader's credibility.

## My philosophy of command

**Be present. Listen. Decide. Re-evaluate. Communicate.**

These five principles summarize the method I developed over fifteen years of on-call duty.

They are based on a conviction: **the effectiveness of command does not depend solely on hierarchical authority, but on the quality of information, team trust, and the ability to ensure decision continuity.**

This experience has profoundly influenced my approach to management, handling complex situations, and working with industrial teams.
