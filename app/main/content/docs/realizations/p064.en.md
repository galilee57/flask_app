# Industrialize data operations with PySpark

## About this project

In a complex industrial environment, the data required for Quality and Process analyses comes from numerous installations and heterogeneous systems.

Historically, their exploitation relied heavily on specific extractions, Excel files, and locally performed processing.

These practices presented several limitations: volume, multiplication of sources, reconciliation difficulties, preparation time, and low reusability of developments.

The progressive availability of this information in a Big Data environment based on Hadoop and Spark opened new possibilities.

However, the data present in the cluster was not necessarily directly exploitable by users.

Their technical structure, complexity, and volume required adapted processing.

**I developed a set of PySpark scripts, tools, and methods to transform this industrial data into exploitable datasets, while promoting the reusability of processing.**

## Objectives

My work pursued several objectives:

- Directly exploit industrial data present in the Big Data cluster.
- Process large volumes using distributed computing capabilities.
- Develop generic processing rather than scripts specific to each study.
- Exploit complex structures, particularly nested JSON data.
- Reconstruct product genealogies across multiple manufacturing stages.
- Prepare curves and time series for statistical analyses.
- Produce datasets usable by Python, KNIME, Power BI, and Machine Learning tools.
- Capitalize developments to allow their reusability by other users.

## Approach

### 1. Master the Big Data cluster architecture

The first step consisted of understanding the technical ecosystem in which data was stored and processed.

I worked with the different components of the environment:

- **Hadoop and HDFS** for distributed storage.
- **Hive** for accessing tables and metadata.
- **Hue** for data exploration.
- **YARN** for computing resource management.
- **Apache Spark and PySpark** for distributed processing.
- **Jupyter, Sparkmagic, and Livy** for interactive code execution.
- **SQL** for querying and transforming data.
- **Parquet** for structured data exploitation.

This appropriation required understanding not only the tools but also their interactions and the constraints of a distributed environment.

Query performance, available resources, table structure, and storage organization had a direct influence on how to develop processing.

### 2. Develop distributed processing scripts with PySpark

I developed scripts to directly query cluster tables and transform data without needing to extract it entirely onto a local workstation.

These developments relied primarily on PySpark DataFrames and distributed transformation functions.

They enabled the following:

- Selection and filtering of data.
- Joins between multiple industrial tables.
- Aggregations and statistical calculations.
- Handling of duplicates.
- Creation of calculated columns.
- Transformation of complex structures.
- Preparation of temporal data.
- Production of tables and datasets for analysis.

Using Spark allowed leveraging cluster resources to process volumes that are difficult to handle with office tools or standard local processing.

**The challenge was to move calculations towards the data, rather than systematically moving the data towards the analyst's workstation.**

### 3. Design a generic approach for processing industrial tables

One of my significant achievements was developing a generic PySpark script intended to process a set of tables from the same industrial installation.

The goal was to avoid developing a specific solution for each table.

This approach led me to analyze available schemas, identify columns and their types, and then build reusable processing logic.

I notably worked on distinguishing between:

- Columns containing simple values.
- Nested structures.
- Arrays of values.
- JSON data.
- Tables with particular structures or content.

This approach also revealed several challenges: empty tables, incomplete data, schema differences, and the need to plan processing strategies adapted to volume.

Developing generic logic constituted a first step towards standardizing data preparation.

**Rather than multiplying independent scripts, I sought to build reusable methods to facilitate operations on new tables.**

### 4. Exploit industrial curves and time series

Process parameter curves constitute a particularly rich source of information for understanding industrial phenomena.

They allow studying the evolution of manufacturing conditions along the length of a product or during its passage through an installation.

However, their large-scale exploitation poses several difficulties: volume, data structure, measurement alignment, and comparison between products.

I developed processing to prepare these curves directly within the cluster.

The work notably focused on:

- Extraction of measurement series.
- Preparation of homogeneous structures.
- Selection of product populations.
- Alignment of curves and Quality characteristics.
- Comparison of conforming and non-conforming populations.
- Preparation of data for visualization with Python.

This approach allows moving from the individual examination of a curve to analyses on sets of products.

It notably facilitates the search for specific behaviors or differences between populations.

### 5. Reconstruct product genealogy at scale

Product genealogy reconstruction constitutes one of the most technically complex dimensions of the project.

In an industrial sector, a product can undergo several successive transformations.

It can be cut, grouped, or transformed into multiple units, which makes the relationships between products more complex than a simple unique identifier.

To analyze a defect observed downstream, it is often necessary to go back several manufacturing steps and retrieve the process parameters associated with the original products.

I developed PySpark scripts to exploit traceability information and reconstruct these relationships.

The treatments include notably:

- Extraction of lineage data.
- Parsing of JSON structures.
- Unfolding of tables and nested objects.
- Reconstruction of links between parent and child products.
- Exploration of ascending and descending genealogies.
- Calculation of contribution ratios between units.
- Identification and filtering of parasitic traceability operations.
- Preparation of data usable in multi-line analyses.

This reconstruction enables linking information from different industrial stages and preparing cross-sectional analyses.

**Product genealogy thus becomes a data reconciliation tool, indispensable for searching the origin of certain Quality phenomena.**

### 6. Build data dictionaries

The availability of tables does not guarantee their understanding.

In an industrial Big Data environment, users must be able to identify relevant sources, know the available columns, and understand the structures they manipulate.

I worked on exploiting the cluster metadata to facilitate this discovery.

My developments notably covered:

- Inventory of tables.
- Extraction of column names and types.
- Analysis of schemas.
- Identification of complex structures.
- Generation of data dictionaries.
- Capitalization of information necessary for source selection.

These dictionaries contribute to reducing the time spent on initial exploration and facilitate data reuse in new projects.

They also constitute a useful basis for technical data documentation and governance.

### 7. Take into account performance and distributed computing constraints

Developing PySpark treatments requires a different approach from that of a Python script executed locally.

Data can be distributed across multiple nodes, transformations mobilize shared resources, and certain operations can become costly.

I faced several issues:

- Large volume.
- Data partitioning.
- Cost of joins and transformations.
- Availability of YARN resources.
- Query performance.
- Complex JSON structures.
- Incomplete or empty tables.
- Schema evolution.
- Necessity to avoid unnecessarily repetitive treatments.

These constraints led me to adapt the operations to a distributed environment and consider their reusability.

Performance does not depend solely on code quality; it also depends on how data is organized, selected, and joined.

### 8. Prepare data for analytics and Machine Learning tools

The operations performed in the cluster were intended to feed various use cases.

The prepared datasets could be exploited with:

- Python for statistical analyses.
- Visualization libraries for curve studies.
- KNIME for analytical workflows.
- Power BI for dashboards.
- Machine Learning libraries for defect studies.

The objective was to separate heavy data preparation operations from analysis or reporting steps.

This organization allowed leveraging cluster capabilities when necessary, then using more specialized tools on the prepared data.

It also served as a foundation for evolving certain operations into refined tables or automated processes.

### 9. Capitalize developments in scripts, notebooks, and repositories

I organized and shared scripts and notebooks intended to facilitate the reusability of developed methods.

This capitalization allowed retaining examples of operations, documenting technical approaches, and facilitating their adaptation to new needs.

I also structured an automation use case within an Azure DevOps repository.

This project concerned the preparation of data required for a Quality process that historically involved manual extractions and calculations.

The repository included documentation and a PySpark operation covering, among other things, data selection, duplicate management, and production of calculated results.

This approach materialized the transition from an exploratory script to a versioned and documented technical asset.

It served as preparation for a POC and potential industrialization, without presuming automated production deployment.

### 10. Share methods and support users

Beyond my developments, I supported other users in their discovery of the Big Data environment.

This contribution included, among other things:

- Sharing example scripts and notebooks.
- Assistance with PySpark environment configuration.
- Presentation of table query possibilities.
- Explanation of main transformation methods.
- Support in identifying useful data.
- Dissemination of best practices for operation reusability.

The objective was to facilitate cluster adoption and reduce dependency on a few specialists.

This work allowed me to combine my Python development and distributed processing skills with my in-depth knowledge of industrial processes.

Building the Big Data infrastructure and ingestion pipelines was part of a collective program carried out with the IT and Data teams.

## Results

**The development of a set of reusable treatments and methods to exploit complex industrial data directly within a Big Data environment.**

The main achievements are:

- PySpark scripts for querying and transforming cluster data.
- A generic approach facilitating the exploitation of a large number of tables.
- Specific treatments for industrial curves and time series.
- Scripts for reconstructing product genealogies.
- Methods for processing nested JSON structures.
- Dictionaries facilitating data discovery.
- Prepared datasets for statistical analysis, visualization, and Machine Learning.
- Capitalized scripts and notebooks for new use cases.
- A documented Azure DevOps repository to prepare the automation of a Quality process.
- Technical support for users in exploiting the cluster.

These developments contributed to making the cluster more directly exploitable by business units and reduced reliance on certain manual extractions.

They also constituted a technical foundation for more standardized and potentially automatable treatments.

Time savings and economic impacts were not subject to consolidated quantification.

## Lessons Learned

### Data availability does not guarantee exploitability

A Data Lake can contain a considerable amount of information without it being directly usable.

The preparation, transformation, and documentation work constitutes an indispensable step.

### The complexity of industrial data often exceeds that of algorithms

Main difficulties frequently concern schemas, nested data, temporalities, and the reconstruction of links between products.

Therefore, understanding the process is just as important as mastering computer tools.

### Reusability is an essential performance factor

A generic treatment or a data dictionary can serve many use cases.

Capitalization reduces repetitive development and facilitates user skill building.

### Distributed computing requires adapted design

PySpark is not simply about running Python on a larger volume of data.

One must understand the distribution of treatments, data organization, and resource constraints to design efficient solutions.

### Product genealogy is key to industrial analyses

The ability to reconstruct relationships between products and manufacturing steps significantly expands defect quality investigation possibilities.

### Industrialization begins with structured development

Documentation, versioning, and creation of reusable components are important steps for evolving ad-hoc analyses into more sustainable treatments.

### Knowledge sharing multiplies the value of developments

A script useful to a single study provides only point-value.

A documented, understood, and reused method by multiple users can contribute durably to analysis efficiency.
