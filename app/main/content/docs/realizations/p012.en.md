# Reduce cylinder changeover time from 24 to 10 hours using SMED

## Context

In a rolling mill workshop, changing the rolls was a significant maintenance operation, requiring the installation to be stopped for approximately **24 hours** and involving **10 operators**.

This duration represented a significant constraint on production tool availability.

An improvement project was launched with an ambitious goal: **reduce cylinder changeover time from 24 to 10 hours**, relying on SMED (*Single-Minute Exchange of Die*) principles.

The challenge was to rethink the organization of the intervention, rather than simply asking operators to work faster.

## Objectives

- Reduce rolling mill downtime.
- Precisely analyze the cylinder changeover process.
- Identify waiting times and non-value-added operations.
- Prepare tasks that can be performed before the stop in advance.
- Reorganize operations that can be performed in parallel.
- Adapt material resources used during the intervention.
- Improve coordination between the ten operators.
- Build improvement proposals with field teams.

## Approach and implementation

### 1. Analyze a real intervention

I participated in analyzing a cylinder changeover with the concerned operators.

We relied on video recordings made during the intervention to reconstruct the sequence of operations.

This approach allowed us to observe actual activities, their sequences, and interactions between participants.

The goal was to have a factual basis for identifying possible improvements.

### 2. Break down operations and identify waiting times

Analyzing the videos allowed us to examine the different steps of the changeover.

We looked for waiting periods, sequencing constraints, and operations that could slow down the intervention.

This breakdown was an essential step in the SMED approach: understanding where time was consumed before proposing a new organization.

### 3. Identify tasks achievable during downtime

We examined the operations that could be prepared before the rolling mill was immobilized.

The principle consisted of transferring, when possible, certain tasks performed during the shutdown to the preparation phase.

This approach allowed us to consider reducing the immobilization time without eliminating necessary operations.

### 4. Explore opportunities for parallel work

The analysis also focused on the sequencing of tasks between different operators.

Certain operations could potentially be performed simultaneously rather than sequentially, provided that technical dependencies and safety conditions were respected.

This reflection aimed to reduce waiting times between interveners and better utilize the mobilized resources.

### 5. Study the adaptation of material means

With the operators, we identified improvement paths regarding the equipment used during the changeover.

The objective was to facilitate certain operations and reduce practical constraints that prolonged the intervention.

This approach relied on knowledge of the equipment and the experience of the people actually performing the work.

### 6. Improve task distribution and coordination

The mobilization of ten operators made coordination particularly important.

We studied the possibilities for clarifying roles and improving the sequencing of interventions.

The objective was for each participant to have the necessary information and means at the right time, in order to limit waiting times and interruptions.

### 7. Build proposals with the operators

One of the essential points of the approach was the direct involvement of the teams.

The video analysis did not aim to evaluate operators individually, but to collectively understand the difficulties of the intervention.

The exchanges allowed us to confront observations with technical constraints and formulate concrete proposals.

This participation was essential to build realistic improvements adapted to working conditions.

## My Role

**I led the SMED analysis of the intervention, working directly with operators based on videos of the cylinder changeover.**

My contribution included:

- Analysis of the actual execution of operations.
- Identification of waiting times and sequencing constraints.
- Search for tasks achievable during hidden time.
- Study of parallelization possibilities.
- Reflection on the adaptation of material means.
- Improvement of interveners' coordination.
- Collective construction of improvement proposals.

The approach was carried out with the operations teams, whose experience constituted an essential resource.

## Technologies and methods

**Areas:** rolling, die changeover, industrial maintenance, equipment availability, intervention organization.

**Methods:** SMED, video analysis, operation observation, task decomposition, upstream preparation, parallelization, wait time analysis.

**Skills:** continuous improvement, process analysis, problem-solving, collaborative work, intervention optimization, operational coordination.

## Results

The reduction was achieved on the first changeover and confirmed on subsequent ones, becoming a standard.

## Lessons Learned

### 1. SMED also applies to heavy maintenance interventions

Although often associated with rapid series changeovers, SMED also offers a relevant method for analyzing complex and lengthy industrial operations.

### 2. Coordination is just as important as task duration

When ten operators intervene on the same operation, wait times and dependencies can significantly impact the total duration.

Restructuring sequences can be just as useful as improving an individual task.

### 3. Operators are the first experts of their interventions

Their knowledge of practical difficulties allows them to identify solutions that are not necessarily visible in procedures.

Involving them in the analysis improves the relevance of proposals.

### 4. The goal is not to work faster, but to work differently

Duration reduction must stem from better preparation, appropriate resources, and more effective organization, while respecting technical and safety requirements.

**This project illustrates my ability to apply continuous improvement methods to complex maintenance operations by combining factual observation, collective analysis, and concrete solution development with field teams.**
